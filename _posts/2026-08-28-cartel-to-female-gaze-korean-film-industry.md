*August 28, 2026 — Gender Watchdog*

**CJ ENM's own America CEO markets Korean drama to Western audiences as "female gaze entertainment." A peer-reviewed 2026 study of the same content finds it "romanticises structural conflicts" rather than disrupting them. Both things are true. The industry producing that content was built, by the Korean state, in the 1960s, on a system explicitly designed so producers could evade scrutiny — and the regulator admitted, in writing, that it declined to police the fraud because it "lacked the resources." Sixty years later, Western audiences are still declining to look past the marketing.**

---

## The Pitch

"Female gaze entertainment." That is how Angela Killoren, CEO of CJ ENM America, has described the K-drama romance genre to Western press covering the industry's export strategy.[^1] Al Jazeera's own reporting on the K-drama wave frames the same industry-level distinction: content explicitly positioned, in contrast to Hollywood's traditional male-gaze output, as centering women's desire and women's narrative point of view.[^2]

It is a good pitch. It is also, according to the only peer-reviewed research examining the claim directly, not quite true.

A 2026 peer-reviewed study published in *Visual Review: International Visual Culture Review*, examining a 23-drama corpus spanning 2015–2025, found that the genre "romanticise[s] structural conflicts" rather than confront them — a finding that cuts directly against the female-gaze marketing framing — and that harassment themes emerge less frequently than the documented real-world prevalence in Korean institutions would suggest.[^3] Popular criticism reaches the same conclusion by a different route: a recurring analysis of the genre's toxic-relationship tropes — the male lead's wrist-grab, the unequal power dynamic recast as swoon-worthy devotion — notes that "female gaze" character design does not disrupt the underlying power disparity; it re-packages it.[^4] Fan-facing criticism and international commentary make the same point from inside the fandom itself, not just from outside it.[^5][^6]

This is not a marginal critical dissent. It is the documented gap between what the industry says its content is, and what independent analysis of that content finds.

## The Real Number Underneath the Marketing

The industry marketing itself to Western audiences as progressive is the same industry a Korean government-linked research body rates as the country's highest sexual-violence-risk academic environment of any discipline.

The Korean Women's Development Institute's 2020 study found that **61.5%** of female students in Korean university arts and culture programs report experiencing sexual violence, against **17.2%** of male students.[^7] Gender Watchdog's own independent analysis of that KWDI data — not a KWDI-published figure itself — assigns film departments a structural risk score of 81/100, the highest of any academic discipline examined.[^7] That is the training pipeline for the industry whose finished product is marketed abroad as "female gaze entertainment."

These figures describe the baseline risk for domestic students. For foreign women in the same pipeline, the documented risk compounds: visa dependency, fetishization, linguistic barriers to reporting, and xenophobia combine to make foreign female students and workers in Korean arts and culture programs disproportionately targeted and systematically excluded from leadership — with virtually zero foreign women holding creative positions at any level of Korean film and television production.[^8]

The contradiction is not that the industry makes romance content. It is that the industry's own marketing language asks Western audiences to read the content as evidence of a gender-progressive culture, at the exact moment independent data says the culture producing it carries the country's worst documented sexual violence risk in any academic field — a risk borne most severely by the women who cross borders to be part of it.

## The Cartel Was Never an Accident

None of this is a recent failure of oversight. The Korean film industry's current unaccountability is continuous with a structure the state itself built more than sixty years ago — one explicitly designed around evading scrutiny, not deviation from it.

Following the 1961 military coup, Park Chung Hee's government created the Producer Registration System (PRS) under the 1962 Motion Picture Law, consolidating film production into a small, state-sanctioned cartel of registered producers.[^9] Fraud was not an unintended side effect of that system — it was structurally load-bearing. The *daemyeong* ("borrowed name") system let registered producers sell their registration status to unregistered producers who could not otherwise legally make films; by 1965, trade reports estimated two-thirds of all films produced in Korea were completed by this "dubious" method.[^9] Separately, registered producers routinely falsified export contracts and forged Hong Kong co-production agreements — real Shaw Brothers films re-labeled as fake Korea–Hong Kong co-productions, sometimes with a single scene of a Korean actor spliced in to manufacture the appearance of legitimacy — purely to qualify for lucrative foreign-film import licenses.[^9]

The state's own regulator admitted, in the historical record, why it let this continue: it "took little action to curtail the process because it lacked the resources to monitor activities that were happening on such a large scale."[^9] Unaccountability was not a gap in enforcement. It was priced in from the beginning as the cost of hitting production targets.

## The Architecture Survived Democratization

The specific corrupt actors changed. The structure that let them operate without consequence did not.

The Korean Motion Picture Producers Association — the PRS-era cartel's own trade body — is the trade association lineage that produced today's Korea Film Producers Association (KFPA).[^9] Gender Watchdog has separately documented, in full, how one modern KFPA chairman built exactly the fused corporate-academic-associational power structure this history made possible: simultaneously serving as a production company co-CEO, KFPA chairman, and a university graduate school dean, years before any criminal indictment — and how his professorship at that university outlasted a corporate ouster and a criminal conviction, and how the university subsequently restored him to the deanship a second time in 2023.[^10] That case is documented at length elsewhere; the relevant point here is structural, not personal. The cartel-era pattern — power concentrated across corporate, associational, and now academic channels, insulated from any single point of accountability — did not end when the PRS was formally abolished in 1984.[^9] It found new institutional homes.

## The Western Gaze Completes the Laundering

Gender Watchdog has previously documented how Western institutional honors function as a laundering mechanism for this architecture — most directly in the case of a Korean director honored with France's highest cultural distinction the same month a mother was ordered to pay damages to the men her daughter had accused of orchestrating her rape.[^11] That analysis named the mechanism directly: "the Western racial gaze operating as designed" — a hierarchy in which a Korean man's institutional prestige validates Western cultural gatekeepers, while Korean women's documented harm inside the same industry does not register as a reason to withhold the honor.[^11]

This piece is describing the same mechanism operating one register lower — not at the level of who gets a lifetime achievement award, but at the level of what gets watched. Where the France case is institutions honoring individual men, the female-gaze marketing pattern is Western audiences and press consuming the industry's *content* as evidence of the industry's values, without the KWDI data or the cartel history ever entering the frame. A festival honor and a "why K-drama's female gaze works" trend piece are different genres of the same act: taking the industry's own self-description at face value, because the alternative — actually weighing that description against Korea's own government-linked research on the industry's training pipeline — has simply not been asked of the people writing the coverage.

There is a third register, and it closes the loop rather than merely extending it. Gender Watchdog has documented the industry's **Normalization-Profit Loop**: the industry produces the documented evidence of its own sexual violence, fictionalizes those exact structures into prime-time streaming IP, sells the result through the same global platform distributors courting Western audiences, and repeats the cycle with no structural reform intervening between iterations.[^12] "Female gaze" romance and prestige-drama dramatizations of real documented harm are not competing marketing strategies — they are the same export pipeline selling two different genres of the same laundering. One asks Western audiences to read the industry's *fiction about itself* as evidence of virtue; the other asks them to read its *fiction about its own real victims* as evidence of reckoning. Neither genre requires the industry to change anything the KWDI data documents.

Readers looking for the single-director, single-institution version of this argument — a specific festival, a specific award, a specific vetting gap — have one already documented in full: Gender Watchdog's reporting on Toronto International Film Festival's 2026 Ebert Director Award.[^13] This piece is not that case study. It is the industry-wide pattern that case study, and the France case before it, both sit inside.

## What Doesn't Fit the Marketing

The industry's own trade coverage suggests even domestic audiences are tiring of the trope inventory now being exported as progressive. Reporting on Korea's 2026 short-form drama market documents industry concern about romance/revenge/reincarnation trope fatigue at home, alongside competitive pressure from Chinese short-form drama platforms targeting the same content niche.[^14] Read against the female-gaze export marketing, the pattern is not subtle: the romance formula persists for the *export* market — where Western critical and academic engagement with its actual content, per the peer-reviewed finding above, remains thin — even as the industry's own domestic trade press documents fatigue with the same formula at home.

## What This Is Not Asking

This is not an argument that Western audiences should stop watching Korean film and television, and it is not a claim that every K-drama romance is complicit in the pattern described here. It is a narrower, checkable ask: that festival programmers, distributors, and the entertainment press writing "why K-drama's female gaze works" coverage engage with the KWDI data and the industry's structural history before repeating the industry's own marketing framing as an unexamined fact about the industry's values.

The cartel that built this industry's unaccountability did not need international audiences to look away. It only needed the fraud to be too large-scale, and the resources to check it too thin, for anyone to look closely. Sixty years later, the fraud has a different shape, and the failure to look closely has moved from a regulator's stated resource constraint to a marketing pitch nobody outside Korea has yet been asked to weigh against the data sitting next to it.
