*October 3, 2026 — Gender Watchdog*

*Updated October 12, 2026: dates and figures revised after a source review.*

**On September 18, TIFF posted twice on X about the world premiere of a Korean film. On October 2, the film's distributor announced that its cast and crew would cancel all scheduled appearances at the Busan International Film Festival, citing safety. In the fourteen days between those two dates, TIFF's X account posted nothing further about the film, and GW's October 3 check found nothing more. This post documents what that silence shows, and why GW reads it as one more instance of the same structural gap at TIFF across its last two festival editions.**

---

Both posts: celebratory framing, with no mention of the film's subject — a real 1974 assassination attempt on then-President Park Chung-hee that killed first lady Yuk Young-soo, and the film's fictional plot hinting at government-official involvement in her death. Nothing in them suggests awareness that the material was politically live enough in Korea to draw a police complaint eleven days later.

On September 29 — eleven days after the premiere posts — a former People Power Party city councilor filed a **police complaint** accusing director Hur Jin-ho and the film's producers of defamation.[^3] The President Park Chung Hee Memorial Foundation issued a statement demanding a formal apology from the production company and the **suspension of screenings**.[^3] TIFF's X account posted nothing about it.

On October 2 — fourteen days after the premiere posts — distributor Hive Media Corp. announced the cast and crew would cancel all scheduled BIFF appearances. Its own English-language statement named the reason: **"After careful consideration, and taking into account the safety of audiences and attendees, we have decided not to participate in the BIFF's opening ceremony or the Outdoor Stage Greeting scheduled for Oct. 7."**[^4]

A conservative activist group had announced daily protests outside lead actor Yoo Hae-jin's home through the end of October.[^5] Kiwoom Securities had halted an advertising campaign featuring him, and a fast-food chain had shut down its comment sections on social media marketing posts featuring him.[^4]

As of GW's check on October 3, 2026, TIFF's X account had posted nothing further about the film.[^2] That check covered TIFF's X account only.

Two posts. Fourteen days to the cancellation. Nothing further on X.

## What the Gap Reveals

This post does not argue TIFF made a mistake by premiering *The Assassin(s)*. The film is a work by a celebrated director exploring documented history through cinematic imagination. The production team's own statement is measured: **"This film does not try to prove a new historical conclusion, nor does it deny or distort known facts."**[^3] The Producers Guild of Korea, which condemned the threats, also acknowledged that "historical events invite diverse interpretations."[^5]

GW does not take a position on the historical accuracy question. Neither, in terms of stated intent, does the film.

What the gap reveals is simpler: **an institution that can caption a premiere twice in one day has the infrastructure to say something once the film's distributor cites safety.** TIFF's X account posted nothing further. That documented absence — from the evening of September 18 to GW's October 3 check, while a protest campaign outside the lead actor's home was announced — is the data point this post records.

GW has documented comparable gaps at TIFF across its last two festival editions.

## Pillar I: This Is Not a One-Off

Across its last two festival editions, TIFF has produced instances of a pattern GW has been tracking: a festival that engages the work without visibly engaging the conditions surrounding it. The grouping is GW's own analysis. None of it is a finding by TIFF, a court or a regulator.

TIFF named Lee Chang-dong the winner of its 2026 Ebert Director Award, the first Korean director to receive it.[^6] The announcement materials GW reviewed do not mention that he served as Korea's Minister of Culture and Tourism, from February 27, 2003 to June 30, 2004 according to the ministry's own list of ministers.[^6][^7]

That tenure ended before the case the Korea Herald headlined as Korean TV's worst #MeToo case. The Korea Herald dates the assaults to August–November 2004 and the criminal complaint to December 2004.[^8] GW therefore does not say the ministry was in his hands when that case happened, and this post makes no such claim.

The question GW raises is narrower: what did the culture ministry do about sexual violence in Korea's film and culture sector while he led it? GW has found no record of any such measure from 2003–04. The earliest government-linked measures GW found date from 2017, when the Korean Film Council was running a film-industry survey on sexual violence,[^9] and from March 2018, when the culture ministry announced fact-finding surveys across five sectors and the exclusion of perpetrators from public subsidies.[^10] That is an absence in GW's searches, not proof of inaction, and GW treats it as an open question. It is the kind of institutional-record question the award announcement did not raise.

Park Chan-wook's "No Other Choice" won TIFF's inaugural International People's Choice Award at the 2025 festival.[^11][^12] In August 2025 the Writers Guild of America expelled Park, along with co-writer Don McKellar, for what it said was breaking 2023 strike rules by co-writing HBO's "The Sympathizer" during the work stoppage.[^13] Park has argued that the scripts were completed before the strike began.[^11] The WGA said neither contested the expulsion; seven writers were disciplined and four appealed, but Park and McKellar did not.[^13] After the expulsion the film premiered at Venice to a nine-minute standing ovation, won the TIFF award and received three Golden Globe nominations.[^11] GW has found no public statement from Venice, TIFF or the Golden Globes saying the expulsion was weighed in those decisions. The Korea Herald did note it in awards-season coverage: "It probably didn't help matters that Park was expelled from the Writers Guild of America last year," it wrote of the film's Oscar snub.[^11]

The Assassin(s) premiere is the most recent instance GW has documented. The directors differ and so do the specific conditions (labor rules, domestic political safety), but the pattern is the same: the work is celebrated and the conditions around it are not visibly engaged. That grouping is GW's analysis.

## Pillar II: Labor Conditions — The Strike and the Award

The plainest version of the labor-conditions question is Park Chan-wook's WGA expulsion.

In August 2025 the WGA expelled Park, alongside co-writer Don McKellar, for what it said was co-writing "The Sympathizer" during the 2023 WGA strike. Park says the scripts predated the strike; neither appealed the expulsion.[^13][^11] The 148-day strike won agreements from the Alliance of Motion Picture and Television Producers — "which includes studios such as Universal and Netflix" — on base pay, streaming residuals and protections against the use of AI.[^13]

After the expulsion: a nine-minute Venice standing ovation, TIFF's inaugural International People's Choice Award and three Golden Globe nominations.[^11] GW has found no public statement from these institutions saying the alleged violation factored into their decisions.

The argument here is not that Park Chan-wook is uniquely culpable, and GW takes no position on the merits of the WGA's finding. The argument is narrower: labor rules that bind the people who make celebrated work — here, a US guild's discipline over a US-produced series — are a second axis on which GW has found no visible due-diligence process at festivals and awards bodies. The work is embraced; the conditions around it stay outside the frame.

## Pillar III: What They Don't Know About the Infrastructure

The Assassins controversy sits alongside three layers of structural conditions that, as far as GW has found, western festivals have not engaged in public. Each layer is independent; GW's argument is that together they describe one gap.

### The body that counsels but, on the sources GW has found, cannot sanction

In a 2020 survey, run more than two years after Deun Deun opened as a dedicated industry body during Korea's #MeToo movement, **74.6% of female Korean film workers and 37.9% of male respondents reported having experienced sexual violence or harassment** (the Korea Herald's wording). The overall rate was 58.3%, up from 46.1% in 2017, when 61.5% of women and 17.2% of men reported it.[^14][^15][^16] The body, Deun Deun (한국영화성평등센터 든든), was launched in March 2018 by the Korean Film Council and Women in Film Korea.[^14] According to the Korean press it offers victims medical, legal and psychological support, sends official letters to production companies after incidents, mediates settlements and runs prevention training. No source GW has found gives it power to sanction anyone.[^17] Prevention training has been legally required on film productions since August 2021, enforced through the Korean Film Council's conditions for supported projects, with no penalty yet for non-compliance.[^17]

**Only 8.7% of victims reported the incident to a boss or supervisor.**[^14] Among respondents, those working in film directing reported the highest rate by job category: 68.2%.[^14]

The pipeline into this industry is arts education. The Korean Women's Development Institute's 2020 study of sexual violence against university students in the culture and arts is qualitative: news analysis, interviews and focus groups, with no prevalence survey of its own. It cites the 2017 film survey above (p. 9). It reports that professors in these programs "are often working artists," so that students "are therefore under their influence even after graduation" (pp. 15–16), and that professors and instructors are "serving on committees that judge cultural and artistic works or projects" (pp. 49–50).[^16]

In 2022, when Venice included a posthumous film by Kim Ki-duk, Deun Deun spokeswoman Choi Eun-min said directly: **"The reason why #Me Too perpetrators are able to return and continue their work is because these organizers remain silent about their association with sexual violence and human rights violations and praise [the perpetrators'] films. This could be seen as an act of pardoning the perpetrators from their acts of violence."**[^18] Kim had been the subject of a 2018 MBC investigation reporting sexual abuse allegations from multiple women. He denied them and sued both his accusers and MBC for defamation; he lost the case against MBC and an appeal, and the court said there was not enough evidence to judge that the claims were false. He died without a conclusive ruling.[^18]

Venice director Alberto Barbera: **"We are not a tribunal. I'm not a judge who can make a decision about the personality of a man or a woman. I am a film critic. I'm here to judge the quality of the thing that is submitted to the festival."**[^18]

Kim Ki-duk's work, THR noted, "was celebrated by the West's great film festivals but tended to perform poorly at the Korean box office."[^18] THR documents that divergence but not its cause. In the same sentence it describes Kim's work as marked by "extreme violence and graphically outré content."[^18]

On the Korean side, THR reports that the director of Korea's movie workers union noted that the representation of women in Kim's films "was always a subject of controversy in the Korean film community."[^18] A 2004 *Korea JoongAng Daily* profile reported that Korean critics "had generally dismissed Kim's films as macho fantasies with a naive sense of morality" and that his films "almost always seemed to involve prostitutes, which earned him the scorn of feminist scholars."[^19]

In the West, the film scholar Gary Needham argued that the distributor Tartan's "Asia Extreme" promotion fed "many of the typical fantasies of the 'Orient' characterised by exoticism, mystery and danger." Daniel Martin reported in 2009 that almost all Korean films released in UK cinemas or on DVD in the previous decade came through that brand as "a certain kind of Korean film – violent, sexual, provocative," and that experts "increasingly feel that these films are being misunderstood by ignorant viewers and celebrated only for their difference."[^20] Emma Pett cautions against extending that critique to audiences without empirical study.[^20] Hye Seung Chung, author of a book-length study of Kim, challenges both labels. Her publisher's description says his films "have been classified by Western audiences as belonging to sensationalist East Asian 'extreme' cinema" and that he "has been labeled a 'psychopath' and 'misogynist' in South Korea," and that she offers "historical and postcolonial readings of victimization and violence in Kim's cinema."[^21]

GW's analysis, not a finding: the gap between how Western distributors and festivals celebrated this work and how Korean critics and the Korean film community judged its portrayal of women is consistent with the pattern in Pillar V, and with what the film scholar Celine Parreñas Shimizu documents about Hollywood, where, as CBC reports, a trend of "servile submissives, suffering, diminutive" Asian women took root in early mass culture.[^22] GW cannot show what motivated any programmer, distributor or critic, and the scholars above disagree about how audiences read these films. The question for festivals is whether Korea's own objections were weighed.

GW draws only a narrow parallel to TIFF in October 2026. Venice's silence concerned a director accused of sexual abuse; TIFF's concerns a politically threatened film. In both, a festival with the means to comment on context had not. Choi Eun-min's remark was about the first case and is not applied here to the second.

### The platform that applies different standards

As of 2025, GW's analysis of Netflix and Korea's Yellow Envelope Law documented intimacy coordinators as a reform demand not yet standard in Korean productions — asking Netflix directly: do your Korean productions employ the same requirements as your US productions?[^23] GW has found no published answer.

GW's BIFF 2026 sibling post documents BIFF's own infrastructure: a search of BIFF's public website returns zero results for "sexual violence" in English or Korean.[^24] The 31st BIFF edition opened October 6; GW found no published sexual-violence policy or reporting mechanism, and no visible safeguard framework for international participants. Its own CHANEL-branded Asian Film Academy selects young filmmakers from across Asia for workshops and industry mentorship. GW found no documented participant protection.

### The legal architecture that exposes accusers

Korea's rape law, Article 297, defines rape as intercourse by "violence or intimidation," and, in Human Rights Watch's account, courts have read that to require force that renders the victim unable to resist rather than the absence of consent.[^25] In late January 2023 the Gender Equality Ministry announced plans to revise the definition, and the Justice Ministry rejected the plan within hours.[^25] Japan's parliament redefined rape as non-consensual sex in June 2023.[^26]

Korea's criminal defamation law, Article 307, allows prosecution for **true** statements that damage reputation. The exception for statements made solely in the public interest sits in Article 310, and critics call the public-interest test highly subjective.[^27] During Korea's MeToo movement, at least one accused director, Kim Ki-duk, sued both his accusers and MBC for defamation.[^18]

GW's analysis: a rape law defined by force and a defamation law that exposes accusers to suit plausibly help explain why few victims use formal channels. The 8.7% figure above measures reports to a boss or supervisor, not to police or courts, and no source GW has found links it to either statute.

**The Assassins complaint:** defamation complaints are now also being used against filmmakers. The September 29 police complaint accuses Hur Jin-ho and the film's producers of defamation "by spreading false information through the film," and no source GW has found names the statute used.[^3] That is a different mechanism from the one that exposes survivors, who face liability for statements that are *true*. GW's narrower point is that defamation complaints are being used from several directions in Korean film and that western institutions engaging Korean film have shown no visible awareness of either direction. This post does not argue they are one system.

Park Chan-wook said in 2016 that he "was under the impression that the Korean film industry had cleaned up well compared to before."[^28] The Deun Deun survey fielded in 2020, published in 2021, came a few years later. The Assassins cast withdrawal came a decade later. GW has found no sign that either has entered the frame of western institutional engagement.

## Pillar IV: What the Film Costs Its Own Makers

The timeline has specific human contents.

September 28 (Monday): the production team responded to the backlash in a statement, saying the film added cinematic imagination and was not made with political intentions. PPP lawmakers and the party's leader were already attacking it by the time of the Korea Times' September 29 cover story.[^3]

September 29: a former PPP city councilor files a police complaint against Hur Jin-ho and the film's producers.[^3] The President Park Chung Hee Memorial Foundation issues a statement demanding a formal apology and the suspension of screenings.[^3] The film, which became the top draw over the Chuseok holiday, had passed 1.63 million viewers as of Monday, September 28, and had passed 1.82 million by October 1.[^3][^5]

October 2: the Producers Guild of Korea issues a statement: **"While historical events invite diverse interpretations, the current controversy has escalated beyond criticism of the work itself into direct threats against the production company, director, cast and crew."** And: **"Exposing the personal details and family relationships of individuals who participated in a project, or exerting pressure on their normal activities, goes far beyond legitimate criticism and can never be justified."**[^5]

The Cinematographers Guild of Korea issues a separate statement: **"If discussions about a movie go too far and attack individual creators and actors, it could discourage filmmakers from trying new stories in the future."**[^5]

October 2: daily protests announced outside Yoo Hae-jin's home through the end of October.[^5][^29] Kiwoom Securities halts an ad campaign featuring him. A fast-food chain shuts down comment sections on marketing posts featuring him.[^4]

October 2: Hive Media Corp. cancels all scheduled BIFF appearances — the opening ceremony (Oct 6), the Outdoor Stage Greeting (Oct 7), and a Lee Min-ho Actor's House meet-and-greet — citing safety.[^4]

October 2 (the Korea Times reported it on October 3): PPP Representative Yoo Young-ha held a press conference at the National Assembly Communication Hall, displaying a copy of a handwritten letter Park Chung-hee wrote to Park Geun-hye two days after the first lady's assassination. Yoo called for the film to be pulled from theaters and for its producers to issue a public apology, adding that he was considering legal action. **"Do not hide behind the words 'freedom of creation and expression.' Clearly distinguish between the historical facts based on actual records and the parts you claim to have created, and explain them to the public,"** he told reporters.[^30] Three days after the former city councilor's police complaint, a sitting National Assembly member was demanding that the film be pulled from theaters.

The film's own makers say they are not asserting a new historical conclusion. **This post's only claim about historical accuracy is that TIFF's institutional role was a premiere — not an endorsement of any interpretation — and that a film exploring a contested political assassination in one of the world's most politically divided countries drew threats against its cast and crew within fourteen days of its international premiere, with no sign GW has found that TIFF's process flagged that risk.**

## Pillar V: The Multicultural Mechanism — Consuming Korea, Ignoring Asians

Minjeong Kim and Angie Y. Chung's 2005 peer-reviewed study "Consuming Orientalism" analyzed three multicultural advertising campaigns and identified a structural pattern applicable beyond advertising. Their conclusion: "The subtle ingenuity of the multicultural advertisement campaign is the way it is able to profit off a multi-racial consumer base through greater inclusion while maintaining White male supremacy through the visual consumption of Asian/American women's bodies."[^31] The mechanism is institutional, not sector-specific.

TIFF's programming of Korean films follows the same logic. The festival posts premiere captions, awards inaugural distinctions, and generates the soft-power halo that makes Korean cinema legible and investable to Western audiences and media. What remains outside the frame, as far as GW has found: the 74.6% of women in Deun Deun's 2020 survey who reported sexual violence or harassment, the Article 307 defamation law that exposes accusers to suit, and a Deun Deun center that surveys and counsels but, on the sources GW has found, cannot sanction. Kim and Chung described their subjects as "not just selling their liquor or their cigarettes or their services to American society, but... also selling the bodies of Asian/American women."[^31] TIFF is not an advertiser. But GW sees the same mechanism: the cultural product is consumed and celebrated; the conditions of production are not engaged.

The institutional context sharpens when we look at what Canada does with Asia at home. In 2022, the BC Government distributed its SafeCampusesBC "...Is Not Yes" consent campaign to 25 post-secondary institutions across the province. The campaign's six-poster set includes one pairing depicting an Asian woman and an Asian man: the only poster in the set showing no physical contact, and the only one where the male figure's face is not visible. He is shot from behind, in partial profile. In the visual grammar of the campaign, every other pairing — white or LGBT — involves affirming touch. An Asian man engaging in a conversation is already the potential threat.[^32]

Canada's Global Affairs Anti-racism Secretariat — the eight-person body responsible for eliminating systemic racism inside the department that operates Canada's embassies in Seoul, Tokyo, and Beijing — lists zero visibly East Asian or Southeast Asian members. Its mandate explicitly names Black and Indigenous communities. The communities whose nationals are walking into those embassies on their way to Canadian universities are absent from the room where Canada's international anti-racism policy is designed. Searching "residential schools" on the Global Affairs Canada website — the government portal that faces Korea, Japan, and China — returns a diplomat's allowances table as its top result: "Residential school in Canada" as a pay heading for the boarding-school fees Canada pays for diplomats' children posted abroad. Five navigation steps from the Canadian Embassy in Beijing reach a page on Indigenous rights. That page does not use the word "genocide."[^33]

GW reads TIFF's lack of visible engagement with Korean film industry sexual violence and the SafeCampusesBC poster's silence on white-male violence against Asian women in Vancouver as the same institutional pattern. Both perform inclusion — international multicultural programming; government-funded consent education distributed in Chinese and Punjabi — while encoding the racial hierarchies they claim to transcend. Kim and Chung document the mechanism in advertising: the scarcity of Asian men in the frame "negates the potential physical and sexual threat imposed by Asian/American men" and "sustains the construction of Asian American women as more desirable candidates to be assimilated when paired with White men."[^31] The SafeCampusesBC poster codes Asian men as faceless threats. The GAC Anti-racism Secretariat contains zero East Asians. TIFF's X account posted nothing further after its two premiere captions, through the cast's safety withdrawal and GW's October 3 check.

The Korean students, workers, and families who come to Canada partly on the strength of Korean cinema's international reputation — the soft-power halo that TIFF's programming actively generates — enter a Canadian institutional framework that has not designed its systems to see them as full subjects. Canada celebrates Korea. It does not protect Asians.

## What Institutions Have Not Been Asked — Until Now

The pattern above — documented instances at TIFF across its last two editions — is not unique to TIFF. The mechanism shows up in how Korean entertainment is marketed to Western audiences: CJ ENM America's CEO, Angela Killoren, has called K-drama and K-pop "female gaze entertainment,"[^34] while a 2026 study in *Visual Review* finds that the series "tend to romanticise structural conflicts."[^35] Western reception tends to filter the product from the conditions that produced it. The Assassin(s) is the most recent illustration GW has documented.

**To TIFF:**
- Did any internal process consider the historical/political sensitivity of *The Assassin(s)* subject matter before or after the premiere?
- Has TIFF made any statement regarding cast and crew safety following the October 2 withdrawal from BIFF? (As of GW's October 3 check of its X account, none had been posted — a documented absence, not an assumption.)
- TIFF awarded Park Chan-wook its inaugural International People's Choice Award. His expulsion by the WGA for alleged strike-rule violations was public record. Did that factor into the decision?

**To BIFF:**
GW's sibling post documents that BIFF's public website returns zero results for "sexual violence" in English or Korean.[^24] The 31st edition opened on October 6. Where is the published policy?

**To Netflix:**
Korean productions. US productions. Same company. Do they operate under the same intimacy coordinator requirements?

---

Choi Eun-min of Deun Deun said in 2022, of Venice's handling of Kim Ki-duk, that festival silence about a perpetrator's conduct "could be seen as an act of pardoning the perpetrators from their acts of violence." The festival director said "not a tribunal." TIFF's silence concerns a different question: filmmakers whose premiere it celebrated cited safety concerns, and its X account had posted nothing further as of GW's last check.

A festival that can caption a premiere twice has the infrastructure to say more than it has.
