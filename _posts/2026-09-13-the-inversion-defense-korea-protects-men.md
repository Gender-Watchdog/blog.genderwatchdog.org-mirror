A Yonhap photographer captured her on December 28, 2023: a 28-year-old former screen actress, walking into a Seoul courtroom, carrying a child. The caption named her hearing date and her charge. It said nothing about the child in her arms.[^1]

![Yonhap photograph, December 28, 2023: a 28-year-old former actress entering a Seoul courtroom, carrying a child, on the day of her hearing. The caption named her charge and court date. It said nothing about the child.](https://github.com/Gender-Watchdog/evidence_repository/blob/master/imgs/sk-misogyny/victim-blaming/yonhap-actress-hostess-clutching-baby.png?raw=true)

The day before, actor Lee Sun-kyun had been found dead in a Seoul park, under police investigation for alleged drug use he denied — three separate tests came back negative.[^2] On January 12, 2024, director Bong Joon-ho led a televised press conference demanding investigation into police and media conduct. Twenty-nine guilds and organizations joined him, including the Busan International Film Festival and the Directors Guild of Korea.[^2] Not one of their statements mentioned the woman in the photograph, or the second woman prosecuted alongside her.[^1]

This is not a story about whether Lee Sun-kyun deserved a press conference. He did. Police leaked investigation details before any charges were filed, in apparent violation of Korean law; his final interrogation lasted 19 hours; he was dead three days later.[^2] The institutional response to his death was proportionate to what happened to him.

This is a story about what happens to women in the same frame — and it is not an isolated case. Call it **the Inversion Defense**: the recurring mechanism by which Korean institutions — entertainment, academia, media, and the law itself, along with the Western institutions that confer honors on the men who moved through them — convert male liability into a survivable or dignified outcome, or simply never examine it at all, while the women adjacent to the same events are erased from the institutional record, denied the same attention, or affirmatively repositioned as the party at fault. Four unrelated cases, four different institutions, the same reflex.

## Pillar I: The Industry's Own Words

South Korea's entertainment press does not deny this pattern exists. It names it.

On July 29, 2026, *StarNews* published a piece asking why actor Yoo Ah-in was already being discussed for a lead role and a rumored 5-billion-won contract — one month after the Supreme Court finalized his conviction for habitually administering propofol, midazolam, ketamine, and remifentanil on 181 separate occasions, and obtaining sleeping pills through prescription fraud on 44 more.[^3] He had been photographed attending a VIP film screening, embracing industry colleagues, without visible contrition.[^3] *StarNews*'s own headline supplied the thesis: *"It's So Easy to Be a Male Celebrity in Korea."* The article states plainly that the industry applies "infinitely loose and lenient" standards to male celebrities' drug offenses, DUIs, MeToo controversies, and sexual harassment or assault allegations — compared to the "witch hunts" female celebrities face for far smaller missteps.[^3]

BTS member RM's 2026 timeline is a lower-severity but concrete illustration of the same reflex operating in real time. In April, *Weekly Bunshun* reported that RM and companions smoked in a Shibuya bar's non-smoking hallway and left the cigarette butts on the floor for a female staff member to clean up.[^4] Four months later, RM and J-Hope were photographed backstage with Chris Brown after his Toronto concert — a man with a 2009 conviction for assaulting Rihanna, a 2025 civil jury verdict finding him liable for $12.9 million after his dog attacked his housekeeper's sister, and multiple further accusations of violence against women that were dropped or settled without conviction.[^5] The anti-violence nonprofit the Pixel Project responded by indefinitely suspending RM from its "16 Male Role Models Helping to Stop Violence Against Women" list, calling the association "careless about the wider damaging impact that comes from publicly associating with a known abuser."[^5] One fan, a self-identified sexual assault survivor, said what the moment demanded: "I am a woman before I am an Army."[^5]

That suspension matters precisely because it is the exception. An accountability mechanism activated — briefly, symbolically, and only after public pressure from fans, not from any institution with actual authority over RM's career. Yoo Ah-in faced no equivalent mechanism at all. The industry's own press said so first.

## Pillar II: Twenty-Nine Organizations, Zero Questions

Return to the courthouse photograph.

The two women referred to prosecution in the Lee Sun-kyun case were a 29-year-old bar hostess and the 28-year-old former actress — accused of blackmailing him of a combined 350 million won. Both had met while serving prior prison sentences and lived in the same apartment building; the hostess had six previous drug convictions, the former actress a prior fraud conviction and two minor film roles from over a decade earlier, before her screen career ended.[^1] This post does not relitigate the merits of that prosecution — whether the charges against them were fair is a separate question from the one this post is asking, which is: why did an institutional mobilization spanning 29 organizations produce zero inquiry into either woman, into the circumstances that put them in that courtroom, or into the child one of them was carrying?

Twenty-nine organizations found the standing to demand investigation into how police and media treated a dead male celebrity. None found standing to ask a single question about the two living women whose case was inseparable from his death, one of them photographed as a mother with no acknowledgment that she was one.

## Pillar III: The Academic Version

The same reflex operates in Korean academia, and Gender Watchdog has documented it directly at Dongguk University's Graduate School of Digital Image & Contents.

On April 1, 2026, three changes hit the department's public faculty roster simultaneously.[^6] Two female research visiting professors — one of whom held the department's only government-linked BK21 research credential — were removed outright, with no public statement, no explanation, and no replacement of their research profiles. A single incoming practitioner with a generic content profile was added in their place. The department's tenured and tenure-track core was 100% male before this change and remained 100% male after it.[^6]

On the same day, senior film-directing professor Kim Jong-wan had all four of his institutional contact fields stripped — both phone numbers, his office label, his room number — with nothing replacing them but a personal email address he had already been using.[^6] His title completed a transition from active professor to `명예교수`, honorary professor emeritus: a Korean academic honorific for retirement that, per standard practice, carries no salary, no continuing research obligation, and no active supervisory authority over students.[^6] He retains his PhD credential and specialty listings on the roster — the university keeps his qualifications for its own accreditation optics while removing his active presence. No disciplinary action, investigation, or public disclosure accompanied any of it. On Dongguk's English-language faculty page, where his name appears as "JONG WAN KIM," there is no title column at all — his transition is invisible to any international reader, even as the two women's absence remains visible on every version of the page.[^6]

Gender Watchdog's prior reporting on Dongguk is explicit that this crisis is structural, not attributable to a single individual — multiple independent testimonies name different people and departments.[^6] The claim here is not that Kim Jong-wan is uniquely responsible for anything beyond what that reporting already documents. The claim is narrower and, on its own terms, damning enough: on the same day, in the same department, two women were deleted from the institutional record with zero explanation, while a senior male colleague's departure from active status was managed as a dignified, credential-preserving, investigation-free retirement.

## Pillar IV: The Question Nobody Asked

Pillars I through III document things institutions actively did: a rehabilitation, a silence inside a press conference, a faculty roster rewritten in a single day. The Inversion Defense has a quieter variant too — one where nothing needs to be erased or prosecuted, because no one asks the question in the first place.

On September 13, 2026, the Toronto International Film Festival's Tribute Awards gala — TIFF's own largest annual fundraiser — presents its Ebert Director Award to Lee Chang-dong, timed to the North American premiere of *Possible Love*, his first feature in eight years and a Netflix-backed production with a confirmed global launch date.[^7] Neither Netflix's announcement nor TIFF's own press mentioned that Lee Chang-dong served as South Korea's Minister of Culture and Tourism in 2003 and 2004 — cabinet-level authority over the country's cultural and entertainment industries.[^7] That is the same window in which a 29-year-old graduate student's rape allegations against twelve broadcasting-company employees entered the Korean legal system and began the decades-long collapse this post has already touched on above: coerced withdrawal under threat, a mother later sued for defamation by the men her daughter accused, and, in 2009, three deaths in one family.[^7]

To be precise about what this pillar does and does not claim, carrying forward the same discipline this post has already applied to Kim Jong-wan in Pillar III: no source establishes that Lee Chang-dong personally knew of, was informed of, intervened in, or failed to act on that case. This is not an allegation against him. It is a documented absence — no public record shows that TIFF's vetting process for a lifetime achievement award reviewed his one year of government office with anything resembling the scrutiny given to his four-decade filmography.[^7]

That absence is what makes this pillar a distinct variant of the Inversion Defense rather than a repeat of the other three. Pillars I through III show institutions actively converting a man's liability into a survivable outcome after the fact. This pillar shows the same reflex operating earlier and more quietly than that: an honors process simply never arriving at the question, so that no active protection is ever required because no exposure is ever created. Twenty-nine organizations knew to ask about police conduct in Lee Sun-kyun's case. No comparable body has yet asked whether an award-granting institution's own diligence process even contains a question shaped like this one.

## The Pattern, Not the Cause

These four cases have nothing to do with each other. A film industry rehabilitating a convicted actor is not connected to a criminal prosecution following a celebrity's death, which is not connected to a university's faculty roster in a different city, which is not connected to a film festival's honors calendar on a different continent. No claim in this post asserts that one caused, influenced, or is legally entangled with another.

What connects them is the shape of the response — or, in Pillar IV's case, the shape of the non-response. A man's liability becomes a story about his image, his talent, his institution's discretion, his path back — or, when no liability has even been alleged against him personally, a story that is simply never asked. A woman's presence in the same frame becomes something to not mention at all — or, when the law is involved, something to prosecute. Article 307 of Korea's criminal code compounds this at the legal level for women who do try to speak: it permits prosecution for statements that are true if a court decides they fail a subjective "public interest" test, which is the same statute Gender Watchdog has documented being used to threaten this organization directly over its reporting on Dongguk.[^6] The Inversion Defense is not one institution's failure. It is what happens when entertainment, the press, the guild system, the university, an international film festival, and the law each independently reach for the same reflex, in different countries, without coordinating, and arrive at the identical outcome anyway.

## What to Ask

When a Korean institution — a studio, a university, a guild, a ministry — announces that it has "addressed" misconduct, resolved a personnel matter, or responded to a crisis, the question worth asking is not whether a change occurred. Something usually did. The question is whether the change involved investigation and public disclosure, or only the appearance of one; and whether a woman connected to the same set of facts received anything resembling the same institutional attention as the man at its center. And when an institution outside Korea confers its own highest honors on a man who moved through this same ecosystem, the question is simpler still: did anyone ask, or did the honor arrive because nobody looked closely enough to have to?

Twenty-nine organizations knew how to ask the first version of that question about Lee Sun-kyun. Nobody has yet asked it about the two women photographed and prosecuted alongside his death, or about the two researchers erased from a faculty roster on the same day a man's retirement was handled with care. And as of this writing, no public record shows that anyone has asked the second version of it about a film festival's gala stage on September 13, 2026.
