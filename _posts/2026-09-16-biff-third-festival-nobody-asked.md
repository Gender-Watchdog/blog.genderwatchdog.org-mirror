Search "sexual violence" in the Busan International Film Festival's own film catalog and the site tells you, in plain text: "NO DATA."[^1] Search "harassment": "No Records."[^1] Search "sexual harassment": "No Records."[^1] Switch to Korean and search 성폭력 or 성희롱 — the results are identical: "검색결과가 없습니다," no search results.[^1] Not a small number. Not a handful of unrelated hits. Zero, in every language the site offers, on a topic-tag search built to return exactly this kind of film.

BIFF — the 31st Busan International Film Festival, running October 6–15, 2026 — is the third major Korean festival Gender Watchdog has tested this way in 2026. Jeonju (JIFF) returned the same wall in April.[^2] Bucheon (BIFAN) returned it again in July, at higher hit-counts but the same underlying result: every match was a staff hiring notice, none a policy for anyone who actually attends.[^3] BIFF's version is cleaner and, in its way, more honest: it does not even return staff-hiring noise. It returns nothing at all.

This post does not repeat the JIFF and BIFAN evidence in full — both are linked below and stand on their own. What it adds is BIFF-specific: who actually sponsors this festival, what its own industry is doing in the weeks before it opens, and how the same "nobody asked" pattern this organization has already documented at Toronto now completes itself, in miniature, at Busan.

## Who Is Actually in the Room

BIFF's 2026 sponsor roster, captured from the festival's own site, includes Chanel at the top Diamond tier, BNK Busan Bank and Genesis at Gold, and — at the Industry tier, the same rank as CGV — both **Netflix** and **Disney+**.[^4] Two of the world's largest streaming platforms are named sponsors of a festival whose own catalog search cannot find a single film tagged for the subject matter this organization has spent over a year documenting across the Korean industry these platforms buy from.

![BIFF 2026 sponsors page — Diamond: Chanel; Gold: BNK Busan Bank, Genesis, Nespresso; Industry Partners include Netflix and Disney+. No Canadian or Quebec government presence at any tier. Captured September 2026.](https://github.com/Gender-Watchdog/evidence_repository/blob/master/imgs/film-festivals/biff-2026/sponsors/biff-2026-sponsors-p1.png?raw=true)

Absent from that list, at any tier: any Canadian government wordmark, federal or provincial. No Telefilm Canada, no Global Affairs Canada cultural office, no Délégation du Québec à Séoul.[^4] That absence is not yet a confirmed decision — it may simply mean no Canadian body has ever pursued a BIFF relationship, or it may mean one exists in a form the public sponsor page does not show, such as delegation attendance without a listed sponsorship. Gender Watchdog's September 14, 2026 access-to-information filing with Quebec's Ministère des Relations internationales (MRIF) asks the ministry to confirm or rule out exactly this, ahead of BIFF's October 6 opening.[^5] Unlike JIFF and BIFAN, where a documented withdrawal or a documented continuation gave this organization something concrete to ask about, BIFF's opacity means the honest starting question is simpler: is anyone from Canada even in this conversation, and if not, is that itself the accountability mechanism working — or just an absence nobody has tested?

## The Industry BIFF Sits Inside, Right Now

BIFF does not exist apart from the industry it showcases, and that industry has spent the summer supplying its own evidence.

Actor Yoo Ah-in — convicted on 181 counts of habitually administering propofol, midazolam, ketamine, and remifentanil, and 44 counts of prescription fraud, with the Supreme Court finalizing his sentence in July 2025 — was, as of late July 2026, already being discussed as the lead in a new occult film and in talks for a reported 5-billion-won exclusive contract.[^6] He had attended a VIP screening of director Na Hong-jin's "Hope," embracing colleagues in the industry, without visible contrition.[^6] Korean entertainment outlet StarNews did not need Gender Watchdog to name this pattern. Its own headline did: "It's So Easy to Be a Male Celebrity in Korea." Its own reporting states plainly that the industry's standards for men's drug offenses, DUIs, and sexual harassment or assault allegations are "infinitely loose and lenient" compared to the "witch hunts" female celebrities face for far smaller missteps.[^6]

"Hope" is not a passing example. By August 2026, a second lead-adjacent actor connected to the same film, Hwang Jung-min, was facing a formal civil trial brought by a woman alleging an undisclosed private relationship and unpaid work, after court-ordered mediation failed.[^7] The Chosun Ilbo's own coverage frames the controversy entirely as reputational risk to Hwang during his "Hope" promotional tour — box office timing, image management, "even if he wins, he will still lose" — with no examination of what the underlying allegation might mean for the women connected to the production's promotional apparatus.[^8] Two men connected to the same film in different ways — Hwang Jung-min as a cast member now facing civil litigation, Yoo Ah-in as a convicted felon attending its promotional VIP screening as a guest — moving through the same festival-adjacent circuit BIFF sits at the center of. In neither case does the industry's own coverage ask a structural question, only a reputational one.

Gender Watchdog is not asserting that "Hope," Yoo Ah-in, or Hwang Jung-min holds a confirmed program slot, gala placement, or award at BIFF 2026 specifically — that would need direct confirmation against BIFF's published program, which this post does not yet have. What is documented, independently of any BIFF placement, is the ecosystem: the same industry, the same promotional circuit, the same VIP-screening culture that feeds every major Korean festival including this one, producing two live cases of exactly the pattern BIFF's own zero-result search cannot see.

## The Same Pattern, Wearing a Different Country's Flag

Three weeks before BIFF opens, on September 13, 2026, the Toronto International Film Festival gave its Ebert Director Award — presented at TIFF's own largest annual fundraiser — to Lee Chang-dong.[^9] Neither TIFF nor Netflix's own promotional materials for his new film mentioned that Lee served as South Korea's Minister of Culture and Tourism in 2003 and 2004, the same window in which a rape case entered Korea's legal system and began the institutional collapse that would, years later, end in three deaths in one family.[^9] No source establishes that Lee personally knew of or was involved in that case. What is documented is narrower and still enough: no public record shows TIFF's own vetting process for a lifetime-achievement-style award applied anything resembling the scrutiny it gave his four-decade filmography to his one year in government office.[^9]

Read next to BIFF's zero-result search and Yoo Ah-in's quiet rehabilitation, the shape is the same institution operating at three different addresses. TIFF did not ask a question about a man's government tenure. BIFF's own catalog infrastructure was never built to surface the topic at all. The industry press that covers Yoo Ah-in's return treats it as a comment on Korean celebrity culture rather than an indictment of the pipeline supplying every festival, including the two named above. None of these three institutions caused the others' silence. What connects them is that an honors process, a search function, and an entertainment beat can each independently fail to ask the same question — and the sum of three separate failures looks, from outside, exactly like a system working as intended.

## What Three Festivals Now Establish

JIFF, BIFAN, and BIFF are governed differently. JIFF is a regional festival with a public partner-logo page that shows real institutional withdrawal when nations that had been contacted acted on it.[^2] BIFAN carries an "Official Partner" structure including Telefilm Canada and multiple European cultural bodies, and returns dozens of keyword hits that are uniformly internal hiring notices.[^3] BIFF, Korea's largest and most internationally visible festival, publishes no comparable partner list beyond a short roster of corporate sponsors, and its search infrastructure does not merely fail to surface a policy — it returns no data on the subject at all, in either language it operates in.

Three governance structures. Three different technical failure modes. One finding, unchanged across all three: nowhere in Korea's major festival circuit does a participant, filmmaker, or attendee find a public-facing sexual violence or harassment policy addressed to them. The KWDI's own 2020 research already explains why this gap matters rather than being incidental: 61.5% of female and 17.2% of male Korean university arts and culture students experience sexual violence, predominantly from the male-dominated faculty structure that feeds directly into the industry these three festivals showcase.[^10]

## What We Are Asking

**To BIFF's organizing committee:** A film-catalog search returning zero results for sexual violence or harassment, in either English or Korean, is not evidence the subject does not belong in Korean cinema — festivals in this same circuit have programmed exactly such films before. Explain whether this is a gap in the tagging system, the catalog, or the festival's willingness to programmatically surface this subject, and publish a participant-facing policy addressing sexual violence and harassment at BIFF events, distinct from any internal staff hiring notice.

**To Netflix and Disney+, as named BIFF Industry-tier sponsors:** Both platforms fund and distribute the industry this post, and the two preceding it, document. What due diligence, if any, accompanies a sponsorship placement at a festival whose own search infrastructure cannot locate a single film tagged for this subject?

**To Quebec's MRIF and any Canadian federal body with Korea-facing cultural programs:** Confirm or rule out any relationship with BIFF, sponsorship or otherwise, before October 6 — the question already pending in Gender Watchdog's September 14 filing.[^5] If no relationship exists, say so on the record, rather than leaving the question to a public sponsor page that was never designed to answer it.

**To journalists covering BIFF's opening week:** The Yoo Ah-in and Hwang Jung-min stories are already being covered as celebrity news. Ask the structural question instead: does BIFF, or any festival in its circuit, have a policy that would apply to either man's situation if it arose among festival staff, jurors, or guests — and if the answer is no, why is the festival's own search function built in a way that could never surface the gap either.

Three festivals, three governance models, one search result. Korea's festival circuit has now demonstrated, independently and in three different technical registers, that this subject does not currently have a place in its own public-facing infrastructure. BIFF opens in three weeks. The question is on the record before it does.
